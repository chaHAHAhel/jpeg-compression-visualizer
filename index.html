💡 What happens next?

JPEG doesn't compress the raw RGB pixels directly. Instead, it transforms the data through several clever steps that exploit how human vision works. Click Next to begin!

2 Color Space Conversion

JPEG converts from RGB to YCbCr — separating brightness (Y) from color (Cb, Cr). Human eyes are much more sensitive to brightness changes than color changes, so we can compress color more aggressively.

📐 Conversion Formula
Y = 0.299·R + 0.587·G + 0.114·B

Cb = −0.169·R − 0.331·G + 0.500·B + 128

Cr = 0.500·R − 0.419·G − 0.081·B + 128

💡 Why YCbCr?

Notice how the Y channel contains nearly all the visual detail — it looks like a proper grayscale photo. The Cb and Cr channels look "smoother" and contain less critical information. This is the key insight JPEG exploits!

3 Chroma Subsampling

Since human eyes are less sensitive to color than brightness, JPEG reduces the resolution of the Cb and Cr channels. This alone can cut the data by 33–50% with barely noticeable quality loss!

💡 Subsampling Modes

4:4:4 — No reduction (full quality). 4:2:2 — Cb/Cr halved horizontally (33% savings). 4:2:0 — Cb/Cr halved in both directions (50% savings, most common in JPEG).

5 Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)

The DCT converts spatial pixel values into frequency coefficients. Low frequencies (top-left) capture smooth gradients, while high frequencies (bottom-right) capture sharp edges and fine details. Energy tends to concentrate in the top-left corner.

📐 DCT-II Formula
F(u,v) = ¼·C(u)·C(v) · Σ Σ (f(x,y)−128) · cos((2x+1)uπ/16) · cos((2y+1)vπ/16)

where C(0) = 1/√2, C(k) = 1 for k > 0. The −128 level-shifts the input to center around zero.

💡 Understanding DCT

The DC coefficient (top-left, F(0,0)) represents the average brightness of the block. The remaining 63 AC coefficients represent progressively higher spatial frequencies. Notice how most of the energy is concentrated in the top-left corner — this is why JPEG compression works so well!

6 Quantization

This is where the actual lossy compression happens! Each DCT coefficient is divided by a value from the quantization table and rounded to the nearest integer. Higher-frequency coefficients are divided by larger numbers, making them more likely to become zero — and zeros compress extremely well.

💡 Quality vs Compression

Lower quality → larger quantization values → more zeros → smaller file but more artifacts. Try moving the slider to see the tradeoff! At quality 100, the quantization table is nearly all 1s (minimal loss). At quality 1, it's extremely aggressive.

7 Zigzag Scan & Run-Length Encoding

The quantized 8×8 block is read in a zigzag pattern — from low frequency to high frequency. Since high-frequency coefficients are mostly zero after quantization, this creates long runs of zeros at the end, which compress beautifully with run-length encoding.

💡 Why Zigzag?

The zigzag order groups coefficients by frequency — low frequencies first, high frequencies last. After quantization, most high-frequency coefficients are zero. Reading in zigzag order puts all those zeros together at the end, where a single EOB (End of Block) marker can replace them all. This is then followed by Huffman coding for the final bitstream.

🎉 That's JPEG Compression!

You've just walked through the complete JPEG compression pipeline. The real JPEG standard also includes Huffman coding after the RLE step, which further reduces the file size. Try adjusting the quality slider to see how it affects the image — lower quality means more aggressive quantization, more zeros, smaller files, but more visible artifacts (especially blockiness along 8×8 block boundaries).
